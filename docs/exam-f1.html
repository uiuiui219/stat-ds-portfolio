How to use: read each question, then answer out loud (or write it down) as though you were being interviewed. Then grade yourself with the 0 / 1 / 2 rule shown at the bottom of each question. The live score bar tells you whether your explanation is complete end-to-end or there is still a gap.

Rubric (same for all questions):
0 — you cannot answer without hedging, or you can only name the technique
1 — you can say what the step does, but the reason you chose it over an alternative is weak
2 — you deliver the full pattern choice → reason → evidence → trade-off → (where needed) limitation and the reason actually matches your project code / report.

A2. Why predict a 3-way group (podium / scoring / no point) instead of a simple binary "will this driver reach Top-10"?
problem framinganalysis (Bloom 4)

Three groups carry two independent facts in one model: who gets points at all, and who is likely on the podium (2nd–3rd vs the rest). A single binary collapses this.

What this tests: whether you can justify the bucket choice using business logic (prize money, constructor standings, race strategy), not just because the code does it.

B2. grid is known before the race starts and correlates strongly with the result. Is it leakage? How do you defend using it?
leakage nuanceanalysis (Bloom 4)

It is not leakage — it is a legitimate pre-race signal. Leakage would be any feature that is/was observed after the race already outcome is known (e.g., race order itself). Distinguishing those two is the whole point.

What this tests: your grasp of the "legal" vs "illegal" leakage boundary, not just the word.
Score yourself:
B3. Weather features (avg_temperature / avg_rain / avg_wind_speed) come from the race window, not the full day. Why that window, and what other window did you consider?
feature engineering

A full-day average blurs the exact hours when the race happened; the race window aligns the feature with what the drivers actually faced.

What this tests: whether you can justify a feature-engineering choice using domain logic, not just syntax.
Score yourself:
B4. Circuit-level features like Race_DNF_Rate, Overtake_Rate are built from past races at that circuit. Could that be leakage? Where is the line?
leakage auditevaluation (Bloom 5)

It's leakage only if those features include data from the race being predicted; using historical averages is legal — but the "how far back you go" question is the real trade-off to articulate.

What this tests: ability to argue the leakage boundary with a concrete rule you can state out loud.

B5. Why did you deliberately drop grid in the final model (even though SHAP says it is the strongest predictor)?
feature ablationbusiness reasoning

Because grid alone explains most of the prediction — the model becomes a near-copy of "who qualified where" and stops providing business insight about established drivers, teams or race dynamics.

What this tests: whether you can say the "less automation = more insight" trade-off out loud.

C2. Why Gower distance + complete linkage instead of Euclidean + k-means?
distance choiceanalysis (Bloom 4)

The circuit features mix numeric and categorical scales, and Gower handles mixed types natively, while k-means assumes Euclidean geometry that the mixed-type data does not satisfy.

What this tests: whether you can state a distance-metric choice for the actual (mixed-type) data.

C3. Before clustering you run PCA and keep the top components — why reduce first instead of clustering on the raw features?
dimensionality reduction

Because the raw seven circuit variables are correlated and high-dimensional; PCA compresses them into a few stable axes and makes the cluster assignment cleaner.

What this tests: whether the defensible pipeline is in your head or only in the report.

Section D — Model selection

D1. Why Random Forest + XGBoost and not a GLM or a naive baseline — and also, why not LightGBM / CatBoost / a neural net?
model trade-offanalysis (Bloom 4)

The real reasons: ~2.5K rows is small for NNs, features are a numeric/categorical mix, GLMs force linearity on interactions RF/XGB capture natively — while LightGBM/CatBoost would add little beyond XGBoost given the dataset size.

What this tests: whether your model choice is a data-driven argument, not a fashion statement.
Score yourself:
D2. Why did you run both RF and XGBoost instead of committing to one?
bagging vs boosting

Two models over the same data let you compare trade-offs (bagging vs boosting behaviour on small tabular data) before committing to the final one.

What this tests: whether you actually ran a comparison as a design step, not just as a formality.
Score yourself:
D3. You turn per-driver probabilities into a final class with a quota rule (3 + 7 + 10) instead of taking each driver's argmax. Why?
model → business rulesynthesis (Bloom 5)

A real race produces exactly 3 podiums and 10 point-scorers; an unconstrained argmax could easily predict too many of one class. The quota rule mirrors that constraint.

What this tests: whether you move from a raw model to a "physically-valid" business rule.

What this tests: awareness that RF/XGB are stochastic and whether you have a concrete plan to quantify that.

Section E — Validation strategy

E1. Why not plain 5-fold random CV — instead you use expanding-window time-series CV per race round?
validation designanalysis (Bloom 4)

Because races are ordered in time: a random fold can train on 2023 to score a 2019 row — i.e. future-into-past leakage. Expanding-window keeps training strictly before validation in time.

What this tests: whether you understand why temporal CV is the right tool for race data.
Score yourself:
E2. Hyperparameters are tuned with caret train() using time-series folds, but reported numbers come from a held-out 2024 test. Why is that not tunnelling/leakage?
train vs test boundary

Because tuning happens inside the training portion (before 2024), and the 2024 race set is held out only once for the final evaluation — no leakage into tuning.

What this tests: whether you can defend the boundary between CV-based tuning and a one-shot test.
Score yourself:
E3. The current code computes Accuracy / F1 / precision / recall macros only — but the report also claims ROC-AUC 0.883. How do those numbers connect, and where does the AUC come from?
metric lineageevaluation (Bloom 5)

You should be able to say that the report number came from a branch of work not in the final repo, and that the code's macro-F1 is its own (equally valid) metric — being honest about the discrepancy is the strong answer, not inventing a link.

What this tests: whether you can be honest about multiple runs / versions and still explain their relationship.
Score yourself:
E4. Why report macro-F1 as the primary metric rather than accuracy or micro-F1?
metric choice

Because no_point is a big majority group — accuracy and micro-F1 get inflated by it, while macro-F1 forces performance on each class (including the small podium class).

What this tests: whether you can quote the skew and justify macro as the right lens.
